You are writing the short written case for the forecast on {{company}} ({{ticker}}) for
{{period}}.

Everything has already been decided. The estimate exists, the distribution exists,
the lens views and their drops exist, the model's measured error exists. **You are
arguing the case, not computing it.**

## The hard rule

You may not introduce a figure that is not already in the material below. Not a
rounded version of one, not a derived one, not a percentage you worked out from two
numbers that are here. If a number is not in front of you, it does not go in the
text. A test checks this, and prose that invents a number is worse than prose that
omits one, because it reads exactly as authoritative.

If you find yourself wanting a figure that is not there, say the thing
qualitatively instead.

## What to write

Lead with the number and what it rests on. One paragraph.

Then the strongest reason it might be wrong — the real one, drawn from what the
challenge stage found or from a lens that was dropped or abstained, not a generic
caveat about uncertainty.

Then what a reader should watch when the company reports: the specific line that
would tell them soonest whether this was right.

Write plainly. No hedging language stacked on hedging language, no restating the
same caveat three ways. A reader should finish it knowing what we think, why, and
what would change it.
